What Is a Dangling Modifier?

An opening phrase whose actor never appears as the subject of the sentence

A dangling modifier is an opening phrase whose actor never appears as the subject of the main clause. In “Walking to work, the rain soaked me”, the walker is missing. Fix it by making the actor the subject: “Walking to work, I was soaked by the rain.”

Updated 2026-10-28

Why the phrase dangles

An introductory phrase has no subject of its own. English expects the subject of the main clause to supply one. When the main clause names a different actor, the phrase is left hanging with nothing to describe, so it is called a dangling modifier.

In Walking to work, the rain soaked me, the opening phrase describes walking. The subject of the main clause is the rain. Rain does not walk. The modifier dangles.

Three worked examples

DanglingWhy it failsFixed
After finishing the report, the printer jammed.The report was finished by a person. The subject is the printer.After finishing the report, I found the printer had jammed.
Having read the instructions twice, the box was still confusing.The subject is the box. A box cannot read.Having read the instructions twice, I still found the box confusing.
To open the jar, the lid must be twisted.The subject is the lid. A lid does not open a jar.To open the jar, you must twist the lid.

A three-step fix

  1. Name the actor. Ask who performs the action in the opening phrase. In “Walking to work”, the actor is the person walking.
  2. Put that actor in the subject slot. Rewrite so the actor is the subject of the main clause: Walking to work, I was soaked by the rain.
  3. If the actor cannot be the subject, expand the phrase into a clause. While I was walking to work, the rain soaked me. A full clause brings its own subject and the phrase no longer dangles.

Dangling or misplaced?

The two problems are related but not the same.

  • A dangling modifier has no possible subject in the sentence. Running down the hill, the house came into view.
  • A misplaced modifier has a subject, but the phrase sits too far from the word it describes. She served cake to the guests on paper plates. The plates belong to the cake, not the guests.

The fix for a misplaced modifier is movement: put the phrase next to the word it describes. She served cake on paper plates to the guests.

Where dangling modifiers hide

They collect at the start of sentences that begin with a verb form: an -ing phrase, an -ed phrase, or a to phrase. They are also common in instructions and in technical writing, because the writer knows who acts and forgets that the reader needs the subject written down.

Two quick checks catch most of them. Read only the opening phrase and ask “who or what does this?” Then read the main clause and ask whether the same word answers that question. If the answers differ, the phrase dangles.
